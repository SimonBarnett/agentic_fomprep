# ASD-STE100 Issue 9 rule guide

This is a concise working guide, not a replacement for the official standard. The source used to prepare it was ASD-STE100 Issue 9, dated 2025-01-15. PDF page numbers below refer to the 434-page source file described in `source-and-dictionary.md`.

## Section 1: Words

Source: PDF pages 45-61.

- 1.1 - Limit the vocabulary to dictionary-approved words plus valid technical nouns and technical verbs.
- 1.2 - Use a dictionary word only as its approved part of speech.
- 1.3 - Use a dictionary word only with its approved meaning.
- 1.4 - Use only the listed verb forms and permitted adjective forms.
- 1.5 - Accept technical nouns only when they belong to a recognized subject-field category.
- 1.6 - Permit an otherwise unapproved word only when the context makes it a valid technical noun or part of one.
- 1.7 - Do not turn a technical noun into a verb unless it independently qualifies as a technical verb.
- 1.8 - Use technical nouns approved by the applicable company, industry, or subject field.
- 1.9 - If no approved term exists, select a short, clear technical noun of no more than three words.
- 1.10 - Exclude regional terms, slang, and jargon from technical terminology.
- 1.11 - Use one technical noun consistently for one item.
- 1.12 - Accept technical verbs only in recognized process or subject-field categories and prefer an approved dictionary verb when it is accurate.
- 1.13 - Do not use a technical verb as a noun. A permitted past participle can modify a technical noun.
- 1.14 - Use American English spelling unless an applicable directive requires another spelling. Preserve quoted text.

Technical noun categories in Issue 9 cover these domains:

1. Official parts information
2. Vehicles or machines and their locations
3. Tools and support equipment
4. Materials, consumables, and unwanted material
5. Facilities, infrastructure, and logistics
6. Systems, components, circuits, functions, and configurations
7. Mathematics, science, engineering, and formulas
8. Navigation and geography
9. Numbers, measurements, time, and symbols
10. Quoted text
11. Professional roles, people, groups, organizations, and geopolitical entities
12. Parts of the body
13. Personal effects, food, and beverages
14. Medical terminology
15. Official documents, document structures, standards, and guidelines
16. Environmental and operational conditions
17. Colors, treated as technical nouns in STE
18. Damage terminology
19. Computer science and information and communication technology
20. Civil and military operations
21. Law and regulations
22. Animals, plants, and other life forms

Technical verb categories cover manufacturing processes, computer processes and applications, specialized subject-field operations, and legal or regulatory actions. A term must be valid in the actual context. Membership in a broad category is not sufficient by itself.

## Section 2: Multi-word nouns

Source: PDF pages 63-65.

- 2.1 - Write a multi-word noun with no more than three words.
- 2.2 - Write a technical noun of more than three words in full. For a long term from an official source, write it in full on first use. Then define a clear shorter form or approved abbreviation when possible. Alternatively, use hyphens to show words that function as one unit.

Count the semantic unit, not only typographic tokens. Do not shorten an official term in a way that makes it ambiguous or changes its identity.

## Section 3: Verbs

Source: PDF pages 67-75.

- 3.1 - Use only verb forms listed for the approved dictionary entry.
- 3.2 - Use only the infinitive, imperative, simple present, simple past, simple future, and past participle used as an adjective.
- 3.3 - Use an approved past participle as an adjective before a noun or after a form of `be`, `become`, or `stay`.
- 3.4 - Do not use auxiliary verbs to make complex verb constructions.
- 3.5 - Use an `-ing` form only as a technical noun or as a modifier inside a technical noun.
- 3.6 - Use active voice. Use passive voice in descriptive text only when the agent is unknown.
- 3.7 - Express an action with an approved verb, not with a nominalized or unrelated part of speech.

Do not mechanically replace every passive-looking phrase. A past participle can be an adjective, and descriptive text has a narrow exception when the agent is unknown.

## Section 4: Sentences

Source: PDF pages 77-84.

- 4.1 - Use short, clear sentence structures.
- 4.2 - Do not omit necessary words and do not use contractions.
- 4.3 - Put complex or grouped information in vertical lists.
- 4.4 - Connect related sentences with clear connecting words or phrases.
- 4.5 - Use `a`, `an`, `the`, `this`, or `these` before a noun or multi-word noun when applicable.

Sentence length does not by itself make a sentence clear. Divide coordinated clauses and layered conditions when the reader must retain too much information.

## Section 5: Procedural writing

Source: PDF pages 87-93.

- 5.1 - Limit each procedural sentence, including a safety sentence, to 20 words.
- 5.2 - Give one instruction per sentence unless actions occur at the same time.
- 5.3 - Write an instruction in the imperative form.
- 5.4 - Put a prerequisite condition first and separate it from the command with a comma.
- 5.5 - Use notes only for information. Put actions, essential limits, tolerances, and safety content in the applicable step or safety instruction.

Test every note by reading the procedure without it. If a reader cannot complete the task correctly and safely, move the essential content out of the note.

## Section 6: Descriptive writing

Source: PDF pages 95-101.

- 6.1 - Introduce information gradually and keep one subject in each sentence.
- 6.2 - Use repeated key words and connecting phrases to create a visible logical structure.
- 6.3 - Limit each descriptive sentence to 25 words.
- 6.4 - Group related information into paragraphs.
- 6.5 - Keep one topic in each paragraph.
- 6.6 - Limit a paragraph to six sentences.

Do not use the imperative form in descriptive content. A note inside a procedure is descriptive content.

## Section 7: Safety instructions

Source: PDF pages 103-106.

- 7.1 - Identify the risk level with the applicable signal word or approved symbol. In the standard's core definitions, injury or death maps to a warning, while damage to objects maps to a caution. If both are present, use the higher level.
- 7.2 - Start with a clear command or a condition that the reader must know first.
- 7.3 - Explain the hazard or possible result so the reader understands the consequence.

Formatting, capitalization, symbols, and additional risk categories come from the governing publication specification or safety standard, not from STE alone. Do an accurate risk assessment. Do not identify severity only from prose.

## Section 8: Punctuation and word count

Source: PDF pages 107-114.

- 8.1 - Use standard punctuation except the semicolon. Replace a semicolon with separate sentences.
- 8.2 - Use hyphens to show words that function as one directly related unit.
- 8.3 - Use parentheses only for supported purposes: references, identifiers, work-step labels, abbreviations, combined singular and plural forms, explanations, or alternatives.
- 8.4 - In a vertical list, treat the colon as the end of the introductory sentence. Count each list item as a new sentence.
- 8.5 - Count a parenthetical group as one word in the outer sentence and evaluate the content inside it as a separate sentence.
- 8.6 - Count each of these elements as one word:
  - A number
  - A number and its unit of measurement
  - An abbreviation
  - An alphanumeric identifier
  - Quoted text
  - A title or heading
  - Text on a placard or label
  - A proper noun of an individual, group, organization, or geopolitical entity

  Do not count work-step or paragraph numbers as sentence words.
- 8.7 - Count a hyphenated group as one word.

Automated counts are approximate when the software cannot identify official titles, fixed labels, formulas, or multi-word proper names. Resolve those cases manually.

## Section 9: Writing practices

Source: PDF pages 115-128.

- 9.1 - Change the sentence construction when direct substitution is ungrammatical, unclear, or changes meaning.
- 9.2 - Use each approved word correctly for its permitted meaning, part of speech, and context.
- 9.3 - Do not combine otherwise approved words into an unapproved phrasal verb with a new meaning.
- 9.4 - Use the same term and sentence pattern for the same item and recurring action.

The main objective is immediate, accurate understanding. A formally substituted sentence is not permitted if it is poor English or communicates a different fact.

## General recommendations

Source: PDF pages 123-128. These recommendations are helpful but are not numbered STE rules.

- GR-1 - Use the conjunction `that` as much as possible when it makes the boundary of a subordinate clause clear.
- GR-2 - Use `with` only when its relationship is unambiguous.
- GR-3 - Use only approved pronouns and replace a pronoun with its noun when the reference can be ambiguous.
- GR-4 - Make the referent of the pronoun `this` clear. Repeat the applicable context when it can refer to more than one item.
- GR-5 - For a false friend, use the word's correct English meaning, not the meaning of a similar word in another language.
- GR-6 - Replace unclear Latin abbreviations with plain approved wording when applicable.
- GR-7 - Use inclusive language while keeping the controlled vocabulary and technical accuracy.
- GR-8 - Use the permitted possessive form correctly. If its construction or meaning is uncertain, rewrite without it.

## Review priorities

Use this order because later edits can invalidate earlier checks:

1. Technical and safety meaning
2. Document type and information sequence
3. Terminology and dictionary status
4. Verb form, voice, and sentence construction
5. One action or subject per sentence
6. Sentence and paragraph length
7. Articles, connectors, punctuation, and spelling
8. Consistency across the complete document

After a rewrite, repeat dictionary, word-count, and consistency checks. A change to one word can change its part of speech, approved meaning, or the sentence count.
